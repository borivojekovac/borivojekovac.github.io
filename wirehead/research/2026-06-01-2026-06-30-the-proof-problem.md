## The agent's most expensive feature is being believed

June produced a surplus of autonomous systems and a shortage of convincing evidence.

The evidence gap appeared first in the bill. GitHub moved Copilot toward usage-based pricing, with code review consuming Actions minutes and new controls for budgets and higher usage tiers. TechCrunch described a broader industry scramble over runaway token costs. The implicit question was simple: if an agent can keep working, who can prove that the work was necessary, bounded, and worth paying for?

The security incidents made the question darker. Google sued a Chinese cybercrime network that it said used Gemini to automate scams at scale. Anthropic accused Alibaba-linked actors of extracting Claude capabilities through thousands of accounts. A lawsuit reported by TechCrunch alleged that xAI fired an engineer who raised safety concerns about Grok. In each case, trust depended on evidence outside the model's answer: account patterns, logs, escalation records, and the behavior of a company under pressure.

The new evaluation market is an attempt to manufacture that evidence before something goes wrong. Microsoft Research highlighted SentinelBench for long-running monitoring agents. Tenet Security launched with a $6 million raise and a system designed to simulate an agent's next action and intervene before a tool call turns into an attack. Patronus AI raised $50 million to build digital worlds where agents can be tested against realistic tasks and adversarial conditions. OpenAI launched an initiative to find and patch open-source bugs. The shared premise is that a model's answer is not the unit that needs testing. Its trajectory is.

This is why GitHub's Agentic Workflows preview included read-only defaults, sandboxing, containerization, firewalling, and threat scanning. Natural-language Markdown may be a convenient way to specify behavior, but convenience is not authorization. The system needs to show what the agent can access, what it attempted, and where it was stopped. In June, safety controls increasingly looked like accounting controls: a ledger of actions, permissions, costs, and reversibility.

The frontier labs were building the same ledger around their releases. Anthropic's Fable 5 was public while Mythos 5 was restricted. OpenAI previewed GPT-5.6 Sol to a limited audience while White House review shaped the release posture. Google DeepMind and partners committed up to $10 million to research on multi-agent safety, including identity, reputation, commitment, and oversight. The technical vocabulary changed because single-agent benchmarks do not tell you what happens when agents coordinate, delegate, retry, or learn to evade a supervisor.

The public response supplied a different kind of measurement. DuckDuckGo made no-AI search easier to reach. Apple made privacy a central claim in its AI architecture. TechCrunch reported that ChatGPT's market share fell below half of the chatbot market. These are not all rejection signals, but they are evidence that AI cannot assume consent merely because it is available. A product must earn the right to become a default.

The labor story was equally resistant to simple metrics. TechCrunch reported evidence that engineering jobs were among the most resilient to AI. Ford reportedly rehired experienced engineers after AI-assisted vehicle work fell short. Anthropic's Claude Science effort emphasized workflow and domain process. HP's OpenAI Frontier partnership moved from pilots toward telemetry, reporting, employee productivity, and software development. The practical lesson was not that humans had defeated automation. It was that high-stakes work carries memory, exception handling, and responsibility that a model cannot acquire by producing a plausible paragraph.

Even Arena's reported $100 million business belongs in this story. A leaderboard is valuable because the market does not know which model to trust. Evaluation becomes a product when capability changes faster than buyers can form their own evidence. The more models compete, the more the industry pays for ways to compare them.

The proof problem is therefore not a request for perfect reliability. It is a demand for inspectability. Can the organization reconstruct the agent's path? Can it show the limits of the context? Can it detect a dangerous next action? Can it account for the cost? Can a human reverse the outcome? Can the company explain what it knew before the incident and what it learned afterward?

June's answer was a market of partial solutions: benchmarks, simulators, observability tools, policies, access tiers, private inference, usage budgets, and human experts pulled back into the loop. The agent can be fast, cheap, and apparently capable. Its most expensive feature remains the one nobody can buy from a model endpoint: being believed.
